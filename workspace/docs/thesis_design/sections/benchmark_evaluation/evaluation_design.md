## 4.1 Evaluation design

This chapter evaluates how widely the dataset represents low- and middle-income countries over 2012–2024 and how closely its borrowing-rate estimates approximate observed sovereign issuance. Coverage is assessed across all eligible country-years. The accuracy comparisons use the smaller sample with an eligible US-dollar primary issuance rate, comparing IDS bondholder terms, secondary yields, rating-implied rates and peer estimates separately with primary issuance for the same country and year. Primary rates describe financing actually raised during the year, although differences between sources also reflect their instruments and measurement dates. Because IDS and primary rates partly describe the same underlying bond borrowing, their agreement reflects consistency between overlapping sources rather than independent evidence of predictive accuracy.

Each comparison with a policy rate includes only country-years for which the primary rate, the alternative borrowing rate and the applicable DAC reference are all available. The alternative and policy rate are therefore evaluated against exactly the same primary observations. Since availability differs between methods, their separate comparisons with the policy rate can involve different countries and years. Direct comparisons between two methods instead use the observations for which both methods and primary issuance are available. Each matched country-year receives equal weight in the error summaries. The amount weights used to construct primary rates from individual bond issues do not carry over into these comparisons across country-years. Supplementary summaries give each country, or each year, equal total weight to assess how the uneven number of observations affects the results. Sample counts distinguish observations, countries and years.

The main accuracy comparison asks whether borrower-specific rates approximate observed primary issuance more closely than the DAC discount rates of 9%, 7% and 6%. This tests their closer correspondence to issuance, not the adequacy of DAC rates for their separate accounting purpose. The comparison is retrospective and does not measure forecasting performance using only information available before issuance. It focuses on 2018–2024 because the grant-equivalent system using these differentiated rates became the headline basis for ODA reporting for 2018 flows, following transitional reporting in 2015–2017 (OECD DAC 2014, Annex 2, paragraphs 5–10; OECD 2019). This thesis assigns the applicable rate according to each country’s DAC recipient category in the relevant year. These categories follow the historical DAC recipient lists, separately from the retrospective World Bank income groups used to describe coverage and select peers. To assess performance over the longer period, the same category-based rule is applied over 2012–2024 and separately over 2012–2017. Its application before 2018 provides a standardized analytical comparison. A separate calculation for 2012–2017 uses the historical 10% convention described in Section 2.3.

For each observation, the error is the alternative rate minus the primary rate, expressed in interest-rate percentage points. The mean signed error indicates whether an alternative lies above or below observed issuance on average, but positive and negative discrepancies can offset one another. This thesis therefore uses mean absolute error (MAE) as its principal accuracy measure, expressing the average distance from primary issuance in percentage points. Root mean squared error (RMSE) gives greater influence to large discrepancies and accompanies MAE to show whether these materially affect the assessment (Hyndman and Athanasopoulos 2021, section 5.8).

The improvement over the policy reference is calculated for each country-year by subtracting the alternative’s absolute error from the policy rate’s absolute error. The average of these paired differences equals the reduction in MAE on the matched sample. A positive value means that the borrower-specific alternative is closer to observed issuance on average. Direct comparisons between two borrowing-rate methods use the same calculation on their shared observations.

To assess uncertainty around these average improvements, this thesis repeatedly draws samples of countries, keeping each country’s observed years together. Retaining whole country histories preserves the relationship between observations of the same borrower across years. This follows the pairs cluster-bootstrap approach described by Cameron and Miller (2015, section II.F). Each of 4,999 draws samples the original number of countries with replacement and divides the sum of their loss differences by their total observation count. Approximate 95% intervals use the 2.5th and 97.5th percentiles of these means, with R’s type-7 quantiles.[^bootstrap-tests]

Shared market conditions and common inputs to estimated rates can also produce related movements across countries in the same year. Country-based resampling does not capture this relationship. A second calculation fits an intercept-only model to the paired loss differences and uses a country-and-year clustered covariance estimate, with HC1 scaling, finite-cluster corrections and the singleton-intersection correction. Tests use a Student’s $t$ distribution with degrees of freedom equal to the smaller of the country and year counts minus one, giving six in the 2018–2024 comparison. Only seven calendar years provide limited information about movements shared across countries (Cameron and Miller 2015, sections V–VI; MacKinnon, Nielsen and Webb 2023). This thesis consequently treats these uncertainty estimates as exploratory and places greater emphasis on the size of the observed accuracy improvements and their stability across the alternative comparisons.

[^bootstrap-tests]: For two-sided tests, each country’s summed loss difference is centred by subtracting its observation count times the full-sample mean. Resampled centred means give $p=(1+B_{\geq})/5000$, where $B_{\geq}$ counts absolute values at least as large as the observed mean’s absolute value. Multiple comparisons use Holm adjustment within each reported family (Holm 1979).
