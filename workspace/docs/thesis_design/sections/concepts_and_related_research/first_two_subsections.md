## 2.1 Concessionality in development finance

Development finance encompasses financial resources used to support economic and social development, including public funds, private investment, grants and remittances (OECD n.d.-a). Official lending, the focus of this thesis, forms part of that wider field. In its economic sense, concessionality describes the degree to which financing is offered on more favourable terms than comparable market financing. For a loan, this assessment considers the amounts and timing of all scheduled payments, including principal and interest. Grants create no repayment obligation, while concessional loans are repayable on preferential terms (OECD n.d.-b, “Concessionality level” and “Grant element”).

A loan's face value measures how much financing is provided, but it does not capture the concession embedded in its repayment terms. Cordella and Ulku (2004, 3–5) formalize this distinction by holding the donor's net financial cost constant. In their framework, a donor can provide a larger loan on less favourable terms because a larger share is repaid in present-value terms, or a smaller loan on more favourable terms.

Grant-element measurement developed in the 1960s partly to address this distinction between financing volumes and donors' net financial contributions. As Scott (2017, 13–14) recounts, Pincus's 1963 proposal deducted the value of expected repayments when measuring donor effort. Ohlin's subsequent work developed the method alongside concerns about the indebtedness created by development lending. The historical motivation thus involved both comparing donor contributions and understanding the significance of repayment terms for recipients.

Concessionality measures subsequently became part of development-finance policy and accounting. Within the OECD Development Assistance Committee's statistics, grant elements help determine which loans qualify as official development assistance (ODA). Under the grant-equivalent reporting system introduced for 2018 flows, they also help determine how much lending counts as donor effort (OECD n.d.-c). These conventions affect comparisons of donor contributions and the incentives attached to different lending terms. Roodman (2015, 399, 403–406) examines how aid-accounting rules can reward some lending terms over others, connecting measurement choices to the incentives facing lenders.

For recipients, the amount of financing received, the amount recorded as aid and the obligations to repay describe different aspects of a lending relationship. This distinction also matters for public discussion of sovereign lending. Assessing the repayment obligations relative to the borrower's financing conditions provides a financial basis for discussing how favourable the terms are from the recipient's perspective (Leipziger 1983, 334–335; Potts and Chung 2008, 1027–1028).

## 2.2 Measuring concessionality from lender and borrower perspectives

A present-value comparison brings the amounts and timing of a loan's payments into a common measure. For a loan of \(L\) fully advanced at the outset, let \(C_t\) denote the scheduled payment \(t\) years after the advance, including principal, interest and any included fees. The annual discount rate \(r\) is expressed as a decimal. Following ONE Data's *Priced Out* methodology, this thesis reports the present value of repayments per 100 lent as the present-value repayment ratio (PVR). ONE applies a borrower-market discount rate to this measure (ONE Data n.d., “The Present Value Repayment Ratio”; Harcourt, Haro Ruiz and Rivera 2026). The repayment value, PVR and grant element can be written together as

\[
V(r)=\sum_t\frac{C_t}{(1+r)^t},\qquad
\mathrm{PVR}_{100}(r)=100\frac{V(r)}{L},\qquad
\mathrm{GE}_{\%}(r)=100\left(1-\frac{V(r)}{L}\right).
\]

The grant-equivalent amount is \(L-V(r)\). PVR expresses the present value of repayments per 100 advanced, while the grant element expresses the corresponding concession as a percentage. The two measures express the same valuation and depend on the same repayment schedule and discount rate (Scott 2017, sections 1.2–1.4; ONE Data n.d., “The Present Value Repayment Ratio”). This thesis calculates these values using both borrower-market rates and policy reference rates.

The contractual interest rate determines interest owed, whereas the discount rate determines how future payments are valued today. The relevant financing alternative can differ between lender and borrower, so the donor's financial cost and the recipient's financing advantage need not coincide. Potts and Chung (2008, 1027–1029, 1031–1034) develop the recipient perspective further by examining exchange rates, purchasing power and tied procurement, which restricts where the financed goods and services can be purchased. These features affect the value of the resources received as well as the financial terms of the loan.

An earlier implementation of donor-cost valuation is Chang, Fernández-Arias and Servén's (1999, 3–8, 11–13) *Effective Development Assistance* measure, which combines grants with the grant equivalents of loans to assess donors' voluntary net financial cost. Their discount rates represent what the lender could earn by investing the same funds elsewhere. They use returns on safe government securities, differentiated by currency, year and payment horizon, without adding a premium for the borrowing country's credit risk. The resulting grant equivalents measure the donor's financial concession relative to those alternative investment returns.

This thesis uses borrower-market rates because its empirical question concerns how official repayment obligations compare with financing conditions associated with the borrowing country. The country-specific financing premia omitted from EDA's safe investment returns are part of that comparison. The currency and maturity detail of EDA's curves nevertheless remains relevant when assessing how closely a rate matches the loan being valued. Currency, timing, payment horizon and evidence about market access all affect whether an observed or estimated market rate represents a comparable financing alternative (Chang, Fernández-Arias and Servén 1999, 11–13; Potts and Chung 2008, 1027–1029, 1034 n. 6).

Using a common policy discount rate or borrower-specific market rates leads to different comparisons when two governments receive loans on identical terms but face different commercial borrowing costs. Valuing both loans at the same policy rate assigns them the same grant element. Borrower-specific rates instead show how those terms compare with financing conditions in each country. Applying both types of discount rate to the same repayment schedule isolates the valuation difference attributable to the choice of rate (Harcourt, Haro Ruiz and Rivera 2026).

For unchanged official loan terms, a fixed policy discount rate produces an unchanged grant element, whereas changes in the borrower-market rate alter the market-relative grant element. If a borrower's market rate rises while official loan terms stay fixed, the repayments receive a lower present value and the market-relative grant element increases. The official loan compares more favourably because market financing has become more expensive. A rise in measured concessionality can therefore reflect worsening market borrowing conditions even when the official lender has not changed its terms (ONE Data n.d., note 4). Changes in the estimated concession must consequently be read alongside changes in both official lending terms and market borrowing conditions.
