## 2.3 Concessionality in official reporting and lending policy

Before 2018, the OECD Development Assistance Committee (DAC) used the grant element to determine whether a loan qualified as official development assistance (ODA) within a system based on financial flows. A loan needed a grant element of at least 25%, calculated using a 10% discount rate, as well as meeting the other ODA conditions. Once a loan qualified, its disbursements were counted in full, and subsequent principal repayments were deducted from net ODA. The grant element therefore determined eligibility but did not determine the fraction of each disbursement counted as aid (OECD DAC 2014, Annex 2, paragraphs 3–4; Scott 2017, section 1.7). The remainder of this section examines the subsequent reform of aid accounting and the use of concessionality in setting borrowing limits and financing terms at the International Monetary Fund and the World Bank.

In December 2014, the DAC agreed to change both the discount rates used to assess loan concessionality and the way qualifying loans were counted. The reform aimed to improve the comparability of donor effort and encourage more favourable lending terms for poorer countries. The decision provided for reporting under both the cash-flow and grant-equivalent systems during 2015–2017 (OECD DAC 2014, Annex 2, paragraphs 5–10). In April 2019, the OECD published its first headline aid figures on a grant-equivalent basis, covering flows in 2018 (OECD 2019).

Under the differentiated rules, bilateral loans to the official sector in least developed countries and other low-income countries are assessed using a 9% discount rate and must have a grant element of at least 45% to qualify as ODA. For lower-middle-income countries, the corresponding rate and minimum grant element are 7% and 15%, and for upper-middle-income countries they are 6% and 10%. The discount rate is used to calculate the present value of scheduled repayments, from which the grant element is obtained. That grant element must meet the recipient group's minimum. The financing must also be provided by official agencies, go to a recipient on the DAC list and have the promotion of economic development and welfare as its main objective (OECD n.d.-c, “ODA flows”).

Under grant-equivalent reporting, the amount recorded as ODA is calculated by multiplying each year's disbursements by the loan's grant element, assessed when the loan was committed. The grant element thus affects both eligibility and the amount of lending recorded as donor effort (OECD n.d.-c, “ODA grant equivalent measure”). The 9%, 7% and 6% discount rates combine a common 5% base with additions of four, two and one percentage points, respectively. In adopting these differentiated rates, the DAC sought to reflect both the funding cost and the risk involved in lending (OECD n.d.-c, note 2; OECD DAC 2014, Annex 2, paragraphs 6–7).

In programmes supported by the International Monetary Fund (IMF), concessionality can affect the amount of new borrowing a government is allowed to undertake or guarantee. The IMF and World Bank adopted a unified 5% discount rate for external debt analysis and loan concessionality in low-income countries in October 2013 (IMF 2013). Under the IMF's Debt Limits Policy, a loan is generally classified as concessional if its grant element is at least 35% (IMF 2021, 53).

A ceiling on nonconcessional borrowing counts the face value of each covered loan whose grant element is below the programme's applicable minimum threshold, which is usually 35% but can be set higher. Loans meeting the applicable threshold do not count against that particular limit. A present-value ceiling instead covers both concessional and nonconcessional borrowing (IMF 2021, 30–31, 43, 53). Each loan counts at the present value of its scheduled repayments if that value is below the amount borrowed, and at face value otherwise. More favourable repayment terms can therefore allow a government to obtain more financing within a given present-value ceiling (IMF 2021, 59). The programme specifies which borrowing is covered and any exemptions (IMF 2021, 38, 40, 42–43).[^imf-pv-rule]

A country's debt position also influences the financing it receives from the World Bank's International Development Association (IDA). Among eligible borrowers, assessed debt-distress risk helps determine whether IDA provides grants or concessional loans and the terms of those loans. These decisions affect whether the financing must be repaid and, where repayment is required, the obligations the country assumes (World Bank 2024).

IDA also sets conditions on borrowing from other creditors. Its Sustainable Development Finance Policy (SDFP) replaced the Non-Concessional Borrowing Policy (NCBP) in July 2020 (World Bank 2020, 20–22; Chuhan-Pole 2021). Agreed policy actions can include ceilings on nonconcessional external borrowing. Under the SDFP, unsatisfactory implementation can result in part of a country's allocation being set aside; if performance lags for two years, the first year's set-aside is lost. In some cases, severe or repeated breaches of policy actions, such as a nonconcessional borrowing ceiling, can also lead to harder IDA financing terms (World Bank 2020, 9, 14). The DAC requires loans reported as ODA to comply with the applicable IMF and World Bank borrowing policies (OECD n.d.-c, “ODA flows”).
