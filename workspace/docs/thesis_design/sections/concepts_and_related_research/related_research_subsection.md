## 2.4 Related empirical research and this thesis’s contribution

This thesis connects research on sovereign market borrowing costs with the measurement of concessionality in official loans. It draws on studies of market access and borrowing-rate estimation to address gaps in observed rates, and on studies of borrowing-cost differences to interpret patterns across countries. It extends existing comparisons of official creditors by recalculating loan concessionality at borrower-specific discount rates.

ONE’s *Priced Out* analysis compares official lending terms with borrowers’ market financing conditions in 2020–2024. It finds that multilateral loans remained favourable relative to market financing even after their own interest rates rose. The analysis uses the World Bank’s International Debt Statistics (IDS), which provides external-debt data including average interest rates, maturities and grace periods on new commitments. ONE constructs repayment schedules from these terms and reports the present value of repayments per 100 advanced. Its borrower-specific discount rates draw on IDS bondholder terms, secondary-market yields and rating-implied estimates, according to availability. The analysis thus provides a direct precedent for combining different types of borrowing-rate evidence in the measurement of loan concessionality (Harcourt, Haro Ruiz and Rivera 2026; ONE Data n.d.).

The OECD’s *Global Debt Report 2025* examines the market borrowing conditions against which such loans are compared. It documents rising sovereign borrowing costs from 2022, following lower yields in 2021, with differences across income groups, credit ratings and regions. The report uses individual securities to analyse both yields on newly issued bonds and market yields on outstanding bonds. Issue yields describe the terms on which financing was raised, whereas secondary yields describe the market pricing of existing debt. Its primary averages weight larger issues more heavily, and its secondary-market comparisons by region, income and credit rating report median yields across countries. These analyses provide methodological foundations for constructing rates from bond records and substantive comparisons for this thesis’s examination of borrowing conditions over 2012–2024 (OECD 2025, 118–122 and Annex 3.A).

Observed bond issues nevertheless cover an uneven set of borrowers. In Presbitero and coauthors’ 1995–2014 sample, 52 of 105 developing countries issued an international sovereign bond at least once (Presbitero et al. 2016, section 2). Gelos, Sahay and Sandleris likewise find more frequent participation in international credit markets among larger and richer developing countries. Participation must be distinguished from the underlying ability to borrow. A government may issue no bond because it uses other financing or has little financing need, but it may also face unacceptable market terms (Gelos, Sahay and Sandleris 2011, 244–246). An issuance-only comparison can therefore omit countries facing particularly difficult conditions, without the absence of issuance alone establishing that they were unable to borrow. Secondary yields provide additional observations for countries with outstanding bonds during years without new issues (OECD 2025, 121–122). For countries without traded bonds, borrowing-cost estimates instead have to draw on other information about financing conditions and borrower characteristics.

Gueye and Sy (2010) address this information constraint through econometric prediction. They first estimate sovereign ratings from macroeconomic characteristics using an ordered probit model, which accommodates the ordering of credit-rating categories. They then fit a regression relating observed sovereign spreads—the additional yield above a reference rate—to ratings and global financial conditions. Actual or estimated ratings enter this relationship to predict a country’s spread, which is added to a US Treasury reference rate. For September 2009, the predicted average borrowing rate for their sub-Saharan African sample is 9.60%, compared with 6.68% for the emerging-market benchmark. Excluding distressed Seychelles reduces the predicted African average to 8.34%. These are estimates of borrowing costs conditional on market access, including countries without observed bond yields (Gueye and Sy 2010, 3–4, 8, 15–17).

Gueye and Sy estimate the coefficients linking country characteristics and global conditions to ratings and borrowing spreads. The rating-implied approach used by ONE and this thesis instead assigns a spread associated with the borrower’s rating category and adds a US Treasury rate (ONE Data n.d.). This thesis’s peer estimates use the median rate of other countries selected by specified income, regional and creditworthiness criteria, with creditworthiness partly assessed through a scorecard when actual grades are unavailable. These approaches all use information beyond a country’s own bond transactions, but a fitted regression, a rating-to-spread mapping and a peer comparison derive the estimate in different ways.

The economic information reflected in borrowing rates also matters for their use in concessionality comparisons. Gbohoui, Ouedraogo and Some (2023) investigate whether higher borrowing costs for sub-Saharan African countries remain after accounting for economic and institutional characteristics. Their study examines coupon rates at issuance and spreads on outstanding bonds as distinct outcomes. An additional African difference remains in their initial regressions after conventional risk controls. In expanded spread regressions including financial development, budget transparency, informality and regulatory quality, the regional coefficient is no longer statistically significant. The controls and available sample change, so these results describe different conditional comparisons (Gbohoui, Ouedraogo and Some 2023, 18–20). The policy framework discussed above assigns the same discount rate to borrowers in the same recipient category, although their economic and institutional conditions can differ substantially. This thesis uses borrower-specific rates to incorporate observed or estimated market borrowing-cost differences into official-loan valuations, and examines regional patterns to identify which groups of borrowers face particularly favourable or unfavourable financing alternatives.

Morris, Parks and Gardner (2020) identify borrower-specific discounting as an extension of their comparison of Chinese, IDA and IBRD lending during 2000–2014. They find less favourable Chinese terms and lower concessionality on average in their sample, calculating grant elements with a common 5% discount rate. The authors recognize that countries face different market borrowing rates, but retain the common reference because constructing borrower-specific rates across their full sample would increase complexity and could restrict the comparison to a selected group of countries (Morris, Parks and Gardner 2020, 8–11, 13–14). This thesis applies borrower-specific discounting to usable original loan records from the overlapping 2012–2014 period. It values the same modeled repayment schedules at 5% and at borrower-specific rates to assess the valuation change on those matched loans.

The OECD Creditor Reporting System (CRS) supports a broader application across providers and recipients. Its provider-reported financing records identify recipients, amounts and available loan terms (OECD 2024). Usable CRS records form this thesis’s main loan application because they provide its broadest creditor and geographical coverage. AidData’s later Chinese lending records extend through 2023 and already include grant elements calculated under institutional conventions. The African Debt Database supplies official-loan records within a wider dataset covering domestic and external debt, extending the comparison across African borrowers and creditor portfolios. These applications partly share loans and sources, including the African Debt Database’s use of CRS and AidData. Their differing coverage permits comparisons across creditors, periods and settings, although agreement between results is not independent evidence when the underlying loans overlap (AidData 2025, 28–29; Manger et al. 2025, 2–4, 11–12).

This thesis combines and extends these approaches through an annual borrower-specific dataset for 2012–2024. It retains the different rate sources separately, examines their coverage and agreement, and compares their approximation of observed issuance rates with that of policy references on matching country-years. The evaluation distinguishes the accuracy of annual levels from the reliability of measured changes over time. The dataset also supports analysis of borrowing conditions across countries and years, including regional and income differences. In the loan application, this thesis holds each repayment schedule fixed and changes the discount rate to measure how borrower-specific information alters concessionality relative to policy references. CRS provides the main application, supplemented by the original Morris–Parks–Gardner records and later loan datasets.
