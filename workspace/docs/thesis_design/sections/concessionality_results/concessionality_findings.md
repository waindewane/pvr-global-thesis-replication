Using borrower-specific discount rates changes the measured concession in official lending, with substantial differences across loans and years. This chapter examines the size of those differences and how they relate to market borrowing conditions, official interest rates and repayment timing. Comparisons that hold repayment schedules fixed identify the effect of the discount rate. Comparisons of complete lending terms then examine how repayment timing affects the relative concessionality of different creditors.

## 7.1 Concessionality under borrower-specific and standardized rates

In the broad CRS sample, the reconstructed repayment schedules have lower mean grant elements under the annual borrower-specific references than under the differentiated DAC reference. Across 1,274 financing records from 2018–2024, the mean grant element is 46.09% under borrower-specific rates and 51.73% under the standardized reference, a difference of −5.64 percentage points. This main comparison excludes records supported only by peer estimates. Giving larger commitments greater weight preserves the direction and increases the difference to −6.41 points.

**Table. Mean grant elements in the CRS sample, 2018–2024**

| Weighting | Borrower-rate grant element (%) | Standardized grant element (%) | Difference (percentage points) |
|---|---:|---:|---:|
| Equal weight per financing record | 46.09 | 51.73 | −5.64 |
| Weight by commitment amount | 47.55 | 53.96 | −6.41 |

*Notes:* Both rows contain the same 1,274 financing records. The difference is the borrower-rate grant element minus the standardized grant element. The standardized discount rate is 9%, 7% or 6%, according to the recipient category described in Chapter 6. *Source:* This thesis’s calculations using OECD CRS records and the borrowing benchmarks constructed in Chapter 3 (OECD 2024; OECD n.d.-d).

Expressed as discounted repayments per 100 advanced, the equal-record averages are 53.91 under borrower-specific rates and 48.27 under standardized rates. This presentation follows ONE’s borrower-side repayment comparison (ONE Data n.d., “The Present Value Repayment Ratio”; Harcourt, Haro Ruiz and Rivera 2026). The average modeled grant element therefore remains substantially positive under the borrower-specific references. The standardized calculation assigns a larger concession to the same obligations on average.

The average conceals considerable variation in both directions. Borrower-specific rates produce a higher grant element for 467 records, or 36.7% of the sample. The median difference is −5.47 points, and the middle 80% of observations lies between −24.78 and +12.63 points. The mean absolute difference is 12.24 points, more than twice the magnitude of the signed mean. Opposing differences partly cancel when averaged, so the overall result understates how much the assessment changes for individual records. A uniform downward adjustment to standardized grant elements would consequently miss both the direction and magnitude of many borrower-specific differences.

The additional loan datasets also yield negative mean differences over the central period: −1.40 points across 68 AidData records from 2018–2023 and −1.08 across 1,176 African Debt Database records from 2018–2024 (AidData 2025; African Debt Database 2026). Their smaller averages concern different financing portfolios, with different recipients, creditors and repayment terms. Extending CRS to the full 2012–2024 period gives a mean difference of −7.37 points across 2,686 records. As established in Chapter 6, the pre-2018 values apply the later standardized category rule for analytical comparability.

## 7.2 Changes across years and loan samples

The annual CRS results show a marked narrowing and temporary reversal of the difference after 2021. Borrower-rate grant elements are approximately 15–16 points below standardized values in 2020–2021, then slightly above them in 2022–2023. The difference returns below zero in 2024, at −2.92 points. Averaging across the two periods gives −9.69 points for 2018–2021 and +0.82 for 2022–2024. The later-period mean therefore masks a change in direction within those three years, rather than establishing a persistent shift to higher borrower-rate concessionality.

*Notes:* Equal-record means, excluding records supported only by peer estimates. Positive values indicate higher concessionality under the borrower-specific rate. Counts identify the financing records represented in each year; borrowers and loans can differ between years. *Source:* This thesis’s calculations using OECD CRS and African Debt Database records and the borrowing benchmarks in Chapter 3 (OECD 2024; African Debt Database 2026).

The small positive CRS average for 2022–2024 also depends on how financing is weighted. Giving larger commitments greater weight changes it from +0.82 to −0.93 points, indicating that the positive average across records does not extend to the average weighted by financing volume. Changing provider coverage does not fully explain the annual pattern: restricting the comparison to six providers represented in every year retains negative differences in 2020–2021, positive differences in 2022–2023 and a negative result in 2024. Recipients and loans still change within those providers.

The African Debt Database shows a stronger and more persistent later difference. Its mean rises from −6.48 points in 2018–2021 to +6.62 in 2022–2024, and remains positive in each of the three later years. The 13.09-point period increase is almost unchanged, at 13.12 points, when only countries represented in both periods are retained. Restricting the records to explicitly dollar-denominated loans also preserves a substantial increase, of 11.81 points. These checks show that neither changes in the set of countries nor inclusion of non-dollar loans is necessary for the positive period contrast. They do not, however, make the African and broader CRS portfolios directly comparable. The two datasets support different conclusions about the magnitude and persistence of the later gap.

The approximate country-based 95% interval for the African sample’s increase is 10.78–15.41 points, using the uncertainty calculation described in Section 6.4 (Cameron and Miller 2015, section II.F). It holds the observed years, rates and modeled terms fixed. AidData’s corresponding interval is much wider, from −10.40 to +21.48 points, and only six countries appear in both periods. Its later Chinese loan records remain useful for comparing concessionality, but provide weak evidence for a general temporal pattern.

## 7.3 Changing market borrowing conditions and official loan terms

The annual loan comparisons combine changes in borrowing conditions with changes in the official financing recorded. To examine those contributions separately, this thesis uses schedules constructed from IDS average terms and compares the same borrower and creditor in adjacent years. Each schedule is valued at both years’ borrower benchmarks. The contribution of the borrower rate is calculated once using the earlier repayment schedule and once using the later schedule, then averaged. The contribution of official terms is averaged in the corresponding way across the two discount rates. This follows the Shapley decomposition principle of averaging across update orders, which shares the interaction between the components (Shorrocks 1999, section 2). The resulting contributions explain changes in these modeled average-term comparisons, rather than decomposing the separate CRS loan sample.

**Table. Contributions to changes in borrower-rate grant elements**

| Comparison | Matched borrower–creditor pairs | Borrower benchmark | Official repayment terms | Total change |
|---|---:|---:|---:|---:|
| 2021–2022 | 65 | +17.49 | −3.00 | +14.49 |
| 2022–2023 | 71 | +1.27 | −14.85 | −13.58 |

*Notes:* Changes and contributions are in grant-element percentage points. Both years must have a benchmark other than a peer estimate. Each pair retains the same borrower and creditor, but describes new commitments in successive years. Contributions average both update orders and add to the total before rounding. *Source:* This thesis’s calculations using IDS average terms, ONE’s repayment construction and the borrower benchmarks in Chapter 3 (World Bank n.d.-c; ONE Data n.d.; Shorrocks 1999).

Between 2021 and 2022, the rise in borrower benchmarks contributes +17.49 points to the mean grant element, more than accounting for the total increase of 14.49 points. Changing official terms offsets part of that increase. Relative to the more expensive market alternative, official repayments become more favourable even though changes in their own terms reduce the concession. In 2022–2023, the benchmark contribution remains positive but is much smaller. Changes in official repayment terms instead account for a substantial decline in concessionality. Both interpretations persist when comparisons are restricted to the same benchmark method in adjacent years, although their magnitudes change. The components describe contributions to the calculated values, not identified causes of the underlying borrowing conditions.

The IBRD comparisons illustrate the importance of official interest rates. In a broader matched set of 36 country comparisons for 2022–2023, including peer-supported benchmarks, average recorded interest on new IBRD commitments rises from 1.65% to 5.71%. Separating official interest from maturity and grace attributes −30.47 grant-element points to the interest change. Borrower benchmarks and maturity/grace contribute +0.16 and +1.33 points, leaving a total decline of 28.98 points.[^ch7_three_components] These figures describe new commitments in successive years, rather than interest resets on the same outstanding loans. Holding each recorded rate constant throughout the modeled loan also makes the estimated concession sensitive to future interest assumptions. A one-percentage-point change in interest after the first year changes mean IBRD grant elements by roughly 6.5–8.6 points in the sensitivity calculations. The average absolute change in the borrower-minus-standardized difference is smaller, at about 1.2–1.4 points, because both valuations receive the same altered repayments.[^ch7_interest_sensitivity]

The distinction between the concession itself and its difference from the standardized calculation changes the interpretation of the 2023 result. In the matched comparison excluding peers, the borrower-rate grant element falls by 13.58 points, yet the borrower-minus-standardized difference rises by 3.13 points. Both assessments show declining concessionality, but the standardized grant element falls further. The increasing difference between the measures therefore does not indicate an improvement in the official financing terms.

## 7.4 How the borrowing-rate estimate affects measured concessionality

Alternative estimates of the same borrower’s financing conditions can materially change the assessed concession even when repayment terms are identical. Across 92 country-year-creditor comparisons in 2022–2024 with both rating-implied and primary or IDS benchmarks, using the rating-implied rate raises the mean grant element by 7.25 points. The mean absolute change is 9.40 points. These differences isolate the consequence of benchmark choice because the observations and repayment schedules remain fixed. Chapter 4’s comparison against observed issuance supplies the separate evidence for judging accuracy. A larger calculated concession is not itself evidence that a benchmark is more accurate.

The African Debt Database’s positive period contrast also persists when a single benchmark method is used throughout. Among records with each method available, the increase in the borrower-minus-standardized difference between 2018–2021 and 2022–2024 is 8.30 points with IDS rates, 11.30 with secondary yields and 14.67 with rating-implied rates. The respective samples contain 429, 417 and 1,083 records. Changing the selected method is therefore not necessary for the positive contrast, although its size remains sensitive to the method and the loans it covers. Because these samples differ, the three magnitudes do not establish an ordering of benchmark performance.

Broadening or narrowing the borrowing-rate evidence also changes which loans can be assessed. In CRS, including records supported only by peers leaves the mean difference close to the main result. Restricting the sample to primary and IDS benchmarks produces a considerably more negative mean and removes almost two-fifths of the main sample.

**Table. CRS comparisons under different benchmark-availability restrictions, 2018–2024**

| Borrowing benchmarks admitted | Financing records | Mean grant-element difference (percentage points) |
|---|---:|---:|
| Primary, IDS, secondary and rating-implied | 1,274 | −5.64 |
| The same sources, plus peer estimates | 1,531 | −6.02 |
| Primary and IDS only | 798 | −10.75 |

*Notes:* Equal-record averages of borrower-rate minus standardized grant elements. The samples differ between rows. *Source:* This thesis’s calculations using OECD CRS records and the borrowing benchmarks in Chapter 3 (OECD 2024; OECD n.d.-d).

The negative CRS average is therefore present across all three samples, but its magnitude depends on the coverage admitted. Unlike the identical-schedule comparison above, these differences combine the available borrowing-rate evidence with changes in the financing records represented. The primary-and-IDS result consequently describes the subset with those borrowing observations, rather than an alternative estimate for the entire main portfolio.

## 7.5 Repayment schedules and comparisons between creditors

A lower contractual interest rate can be offset by earlier principal repayments, changing which creditor’s terms provide the larger concession. Among 177 borrower-year creditor pairs with benchmarks other than peers, the ordering based on interest rates reverses under complete repayment valuation in 17 cases. The median absolute grant-element difference among those reversals is 5.68 points. Including peer-supported comparisons gives 19 reversals among 209 pairs. These results use schedules constructed from the creditor-specific average terms in IDS, following ONE’s approach (World Bank n.d.-c; ONE Data n.d., “The debt service schedule”).

Côte d’Ivoire in 2024 illustrates the mechanism. The recorded average interest rate is lower for Chinese lending than for IDA, at 2.00% against 2.25%. Chinese maturity is approximately 19.9 years, compared with 27.5 years for IDA, and the average grace period is also shorter. Valuing both schedules with the same primary borrowing benchmark gives grant elements of 47.49% for China and 53.17% for IDA. The lower Chinese interest rate does not compensate for the earlier repayments. Interest-rate comparisons alone would therefore give the opposite ordering for these average terms.

Across common borrowers and years in 2018–2024, the Chinese average-term scenarios have lower mean modeled grant elements than the IBRD and IDA scenarios by 13.88 and 24.29 points, respectively, when peer benchmarks are excluded. These comparisons contain 62 and 67 borrower-year pairs. IBRD’s mean grant element is 8.66 points below IDA’s across 48 matched pairs, although IBRD has the higher value in 21 of them. The averages thus describe tendencies within the recorded portfolios, with considerable variation among borrowers. Matching the borrower and year gives both creditors the same discount rate, but does not match their projects or lending purposes.

The original records used by Morris, Parks and Gardner allow a separate examination of how borrower-specific discounting changes an established creditor comparison. Their study uses a fixed 5% rate and explicitly discusses the scope for borrower-specific rates (Morris, Parks and Gardner 2020, 8–9). Across 462 usable records from the overlapping 2012–2014 period, excluding peer-only benchmarks, replacing 5% with borrower-specific rates raises the mean grant element by 2.95 points. Both calculations use the same reconstructed repayment schedules, so that difference is separate from the effect of reconstructing loan duration described in Chapter 6.

For borrowers receiving financing from both creditors in the same year, the China-minus-IBRD difference changes from −18.85 points at 5% to −17.98 under borrower-specific rates across eight borrower-years. The China-minus-IDA difference changes very little across nine borrower-years. Within this small matched sample, incorporating borrower conditions changes the measured concession without overturning the average Chinese–World Bank ordering. The extension therefore adds information about how favourable the terms are relative to recipients’ financing conditions, while retaining the direction of the creditor comparison on the observations that can be matched.
