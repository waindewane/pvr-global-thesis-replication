# 8. Discussion and conclusion

## 8.1 Concessionality, additionality and development effectiveness

Research on development effectiveness asks what financing achieves beyond what would have happened without the intervention. In the additionality literature, Carter, Van de Sijpe and Calel (2021, section 2.2) examine whether development finance institutions enable investment that would otherwise not occur. Their analysis concerns investment in private-sector projects, but the underlying question also motivates scrutiny of sovereign development lending: what does official financing enable the recipient to do that its other financing options would not? Assessing the financial advantage offered to the borrower is one part of investigating that question.

Mutambatsere and Schellekens (2020, 6–8, 15, 29) connect this question explicitly to concessionality. In their analysis of blended finance, they distinguish the development value of a project, the additional contribution of a development finance institution and the need for concessional terms. A project may produce worthwhile outcomes without requiring subsidized financing, and an institution may add value through expertise or access to finance without offering a financial concession. The effectiveness question therefore concerns what the concessional component itself enables and how much is needed. Although their framework addresses private investment, it provides a useful basis for asking how favourable sovereign lending terms contribute to development outcomes.

This thesis contributes to that research by comparing the present value of modeled official repayments under borrower-specific annual references across a broad country-year panel. The results show why nominal loan amounts or standardized grant elements alone can give an incomplete account of the concession received. The resulting differences show how much the assessed concession can depend on the borrowing reference and repayment assumptions. The panel provides a starting point for those comparisons under substantial constraints on data availability and comparability.

Linking this panel to development outcomes would allow further research to ask whether more concessional financing produces larger additional gains, and how those gains compare per unit of concession. Establishing those effects would require outcome data and a design that separates the consequences of the financing terms from the conditions that led to those terms. The financial denominator would also need to match the research question: a borrower-side concession measures the recipient's financing advantage, whereas effectiveness per unit of public expenditure requires the donor's cost (Potts and Chung 2008, 1027–1029).

## 8.2 Public communication and aid accounting

Expressing the present value of repayments per 100 advanced gives journalists, civil-society organisations and other non-specialist audiences an accessible way to compare sovereign lending terms. This thesis follows ONE's presentation of the borrower-side comparison (ONE Data n.d.; Harcourt, Haro Ruiz and Rivera 2026). Stated alongside the loan amount and borrowing benchmark, the repayment value makes the degree of concessionality more concrete than a financing-volume announcement alone. This can give public discussion of lender–borrower relations a clearer financial basis, with the underlying terms and reference rate available for scrutiny.

If accounting rules assigned lower grant equivalents to eligible loans, recorded ODA would fall for unchanged disbursements and increase the shortfall of a donor already below the 0.7% of gross national income target (OECD n.d.-c; n.d.-e). The main CRS comparison produces lower average grant elements when borrower-specific rates replace the differentiated policy rates, without recalculating individual donors' ODA totals.

## 8.3 Limits of interpretation and priorities for further research

The accuracy evidence is strongest for countries that issue bonds, because their observed issuance rates provide the basis for evaluation. The dataset also contains estimates for countries without those observations, including borrowers whose financing alternatives may be more restricted. Performance among observed issuers therefore provides incomplete evidence about accuracy for the countries most dependent on estimates. Peer medians can themselves rely heavily on rating-implied inputs and combine rates with different timing and currency coverage; their additional coverage is not independent market evidence. Validation on later borrowing observations for previously unobserved countries would strengthen the basis for using these estimates. This thesis holds observed or estimated borrowing conditions fixed when evaluating official repayments. Assessing whether a government could replace a substantial volume of official finance with market borrowing would require evidence on financing availability, instrument terms and how borrowing costs respond to additional debt.

Closer matching between each loan and its borrowing benchmark would improve the comparison of financing terms. The annual rates used here do not reproduce every loan's currency, maturity and commitment date, and the broad CRS records do not establish contractual currency. The 5.64-point mean difference is consequently a result under the common-currency, annual-rate and repayment assumptions, not an estimate of the gap from fully matched market financing for each loan. Chang, Fernández-Arias and Servén (1999, 11–13) used currency- and maturity-specific government-security yield curves to measure donors' financial concessions. An extension could combine that detail with borrower-specific risk premia and information available at commitment. Each payment would then be valued using a discount factor matched to its currency and horizon. More complete contractual data would also permit actual disbursement schedules, fees and variable interest payments to replace the present assumptions. The interest-rate sensitivities in Chapter 7 show that repayment assumptions can materially affect grant-element levels, although their effect on the difference between two valuations of the same schedule is smaller in that exercise.

Two implementation choices also require further assessment: the rating estimate maps foreign-currency grades to spread groups based on local-currency ratings, and the repayment calculations use quoted bond yields without harmonizing their compounding conventions. Further research should align the rating concepts and convert yields to a common effective annual basis before aggregation and discounting. The secondary series would also benefit from dated historical outstanding balances and a common date for measuring remaining maturity across its two calculation routes. The effect of these changes on the headline concessionality difference has not been quantified.

The results also depend on which financing records contain usable terms and can be matched to a borrower benchmark. They describe those portfolios, with common-country and common-provider comparisons testing specific changes in composition. The analysis does not reweight the sample to represent excluded CRS lending. Comparing included and excluded records by provider, recipient risk and loan terms would help establish the direction of that selection. The reported country-based uncertainty intervals account for dependence among observations from the same country, following the reasoning discussed by Cameron and Miller (2015), but hold the benchmark estimates and modeled repayments fixed. They consequently leave uncertainty about those inputs outside the intervals. Shared rate inputs, the short span of calendar years and loans appearing in more than one dataset also limit the independent evidence available. A further extension would propagate alternative benchmark and repayment assumptions jointly through the calculations. The present separate sensitivities do not establish a probability that the headline difference or each creditor ordering retains its sign under that combined uncertainty.

## 8.4 Conclusion

This thesis constructs borrower-specific sovereign borrowing benchmarks for 2012–2024 by combining observed financing terms and market yields with estimates for countries lacking direct observations. Retaining the sources and assumptions makes it possible to examine both coverage and comparability. In the matched 2018–2024 issuance samples, all four alternatives to primary yields have a lower mean absolute error than the standardized policy reference, although the strength of the evidence varies across methods. The evaluation provides stronger support for comparing annual borrowing-rate levels than for measuring changes from one year to the next. These results establish both the usefulness of borrower-specific information and the limits of treating the resulting rates as interchangeable observations.

Using those annual benchmarks changes the concessionality calculated from the reconstructed official repayment schedules. In the main CRS comparison for 2018–2024, the mean grant element is 5.64 percentage points lower than under the differentiated policy rates. Substantial differences across financing records and years mean that this average cannot serve as a uniform adjustment. The findings also show how changes in official lending terms and the timing of repayments affect the financing advantage and comparisons between creditors. By constructing and evaluating the borrowing benchmarks, then applying them to recorded official lending terms, this thesis makes recipients' financing conditions explicit in concessionality measurement. The resulting evidence provides a basis for research on the effectiveness of concessional finance and for clearer public comparisons of sovereign lending terms.
