This thesis examines how official-loan concessionality changes when repayments are assessed against borrowers' market financing conditions rather than the OECD's standardized discount rates of 9%, 7% or 6%, which depend on recipient category. It constructs a sovereign borrowing-rate dataset for 2012–2024, combining bond yields, reported borrowing terms and estimates based on credit ratings or comparable countries. The dataset supplies a reference for 1,701 of 1,756 low- and middle-income country-years, with estimates accounting for much of the coverage. In matched 2018–2024 samples, rates from outstanding bonds, reported borrowing terms, credit ratings and comparable countries each approximate observed issuance more closely, on average, than policy rates. Performance in measuring annual changes is weaker. The loan analysis uses reconstructed repayments and annual rates under a common-currency assumption, holding schedules fixed to compare grant elements, defined as the difference between the advance and the present value of repayments as a percentage of the advance. Across 1,274 usable OECD Creditor Reporting System financing records from 2018–2024, excluding records whose benchmark relies solely on comparable countries, the mean grant element falls from 51.73% under policy rates to 46.09% under borrower-specific rates. Borrower-specific rates nevertheless produce higher grant elements in 36.7% of records. Additional creditor comparisons show that repayment timing can reverse the ordering suggested by contractual interest rates. The findings establish the importance of borrower financing conditions for measuring concessionality and provide a basis for further research on development-finance effectiveness.
