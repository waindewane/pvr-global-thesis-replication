Official development loans differ in the financial advantage they offer recipients. A loan's amount describes the resources committed, but its interest payments and repayment dates determine the obligations attached to those resources. Concessionality measures how favourable those terms are relative to a specified financing alternative. The grant element expresses the difference between the amount advanced and the present value of repayments as a percentage of the advance. Its value therefore depends on the discount rate used to assess those repayments (Scott 2017; Potts and Chung 2008).

Official aid statistics use standardized discount rates to calculate grant elements. Under the OECD Development Assistance Committee's grant-equivalent rules for sovereign loans, these rates are 9%, 7% or 6%, depending on the recipient category (OECD n.d.-c). These rules became the headline basis for official development assistance reporting for 2018 flows, which is why the main institutional comparison in this thesis covers 2018–2024 (OECD 2019). Countries within a recipient category can nevertheless face very different market borrowing costs (OECD 2025, 118–122). From the recipient's perspective, the relevant comparison concerns how the official loan terms compare with its own financing alternatives. Establishing that comparison would give assessments of development lending a more specific financial basis, including public discussion of the terms offered by different creditors.

This thesis asks two linked questions: how can transparent, borrower-specific sovereign borrowing benchmarks be constructed for 2012–2024, and how does using them change measured concessionality relative to policy reference rates? Here, a borrowing benchmark is an observed or estimated annual interest-rate reference intended to approximate a government's borrowing conditions. The loan comparisons value reconstructed repayments under these references; they do not establish that equivalent market financing was available at each loan's currency, maturity and commitment date. Constructing these rates is a substantial part of the research because many countries do not issue international bonds every year, and available borrowing information differs in its timing, instrument coverage and completeness. Studies of market participation show that observed issuers are a selected group of developing countries (Gelos, Sahay and Sandleris 2011; Presbitero et al. 2016).

The approach builds on the OECD's analysis of sovereign bond markets and ONE's comparison of official repayments with borrower financing conditions (OECD 2025; Harcourt, Haro Ruiz and Rivera 2026; ONE Data n.d.). This thesis constructs an annual dataset combining yields on newly issued and outstanding bonds, World Bank reports of interest rates on new external borrowing, and estimates based on sovereign credit ratings or comparable countries. It preserves each source separately and evaluates the alternative rates against observed issuance on matching countries and years. It then uses the dataset to examine borrowing conditions across countries and over time, and to recalculate grant elements for official loans. The OECD Creditor Reporting System (CRS) supplies the broadest loan sample, supplemented by the records used by Morris, Parks and Gardner (2020), later AidData records and the African Debt Database (AidData 2025; African Debt Database 2026). Within each loan comparison, the repayment schedule remains fixed and only the discount rate changes.

The constructed dataset provides a borrowing reference for 1,701 of 1,756 low- and middle-income country-years. Much of this coverage depends on estimates, particularly for low-income countries. In matched comparisons for 2018–2024, rates from outstanding bonds, reported new external borrowing, credit ratings and comparable countries each approximate yields on newly issued bonds more closely, on average, than the differentiated policy rates. Evidence is stronger for approximating annual rate levels than for measuring changes between years. The country comparisons show a widespread increase in borrowing costs in 2022, accompanied by wider differences between lower- and higher-cost borrowers. These findings support using borrower-specific information while showing why the source and composition of each comparison need to remain visible.

For 1,274 usable CRS financing records from 2018–2024, excluding records whose borrowing benchmark is based solely on comparable countries, the mean grant element falls from 51.73% under the differentiated policy rates to 46.09% under borrower-specific rates. The difference of 5.64 percentage points conceals substantial variation: borrower-specific rates produce a higher grant element in 36.7% of records. The later-year results also differ across creditor portfolios, and the timing of repayments reverses some creditor orderings based on contractual interest rates alone. These results provide a basis for examining concessionality in research on development effectiveness, without treating the financial concession itself as evidence of additional development outcomes.

Chapter 2 develops the concepts, institutional uses and related research. Chapter 3 explains construction of the borrowing-rate dataset, Chapter 4 evaluates its coverage and accuracy, and Chapter 5 examines borrowing conditions across countries and years. Chapter 6 sets out the official-loan data and repayment calculations, followed by the concessionality findings in Chapter 7. Chapter 8 discusses their implications, limitations and contribution to understanding development finance.
